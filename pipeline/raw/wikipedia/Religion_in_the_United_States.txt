The aftermath led to what historian [[Martin Marty]] calls the "Evangelical Empire", a period in which evangelicals dominated US cultural institutions.{{sfn|Marty|1986|loc=Front matter}} They influenced measures to [[Abolitionism in the United States|abolish slavery]], further [[women's rights]], enact [[Prohibition in the United States|prohibition]], and reform [[History of education in the United States|education]] and [[History of United States prison systems|criminal justice]].<ref>{{Cite web |last=Conroy-Krutz |first=Emily |date=June 7, 2013 |title=Religion and Reform |url=https://www.americanyawp.com/text/10-religion-and-reform/ |access-date=2023-03-19 |website=The American Yawp}}</ref> New denominations were formed ([[Adventism]], [[Jehovah's Witnesses]], the [[Latter Day Saint movement]] (Mormonism), [[Churches of Christ]] and [[Church of Christ, Scientist]], [[Unitarianism|Unitarian]] and [[Universalist]], [[Pentecostalism]]).{{sfn|Backman|1983|loc=}} Outside of Protestantism, an unprecedented number of Catholic and [[Jewish]] immigrants arrived in the United States during the immigrant waves of the mid to late 19th and 20th century.

==History==
{{Main|History of religion in the United States}}
[[File:George-Henry-Boughton-Pilgrims-Going-To-Church.jpg|thumb|''[[Pilgrims Going to Church]]'' by [[George Henry Boughton]] (1867)]]

Ever since its early colonial days, when some Protestant [[dissenter]] English and German settlers moved in search of [[Freedom of religion|religious freedom]], America has been profoundly influenced by religion.<ref>Sydney Ahlstrom, ''A Religious History of the American People'' (Yale UP, 2nd ed. 2004) {{ISBN|0-300-10012-4}}</ref> Throughout its history, religious involvement among American citizens has grown since 1776 from 17% of the US population to 62% in 2000.<ref name="Stark churching">{{cite book|title=The Churching of America, 1776–2005: Winners and Losers in our Religious Economy|last1=Finke|first1=Roger|last2=Stark|first2=Rodney|date=2006|publisher=Rutgers University Press|isbn=978-0813535531|location=New Brunswick, NJ|pages=22, 23}}</ref> Approximately 35-40 percent of Americans regularly attended religious services from eighteenth-century colonial America up to 1940.<ref name="brooks2"/> That influence continues in American culture, social life, and politics.<ref>Kevin M. Schultz, and Paul Harvey, "Everywhere and Nowhere: Recent Trends in American Religious History and Historiography", ''Journal of the American Academy of Religion'', March 2010, Vol. 78 Issue 1, pp. 129–162</ref> Several of the original [[Thirteen Colonies]] were established by settlers who wished to practice their own religion within a community of like-minded people: the Massachusetts Bay Colony was established by English [[Puritan]]s (Congregationalists), Pennsylvania by British [[Religious Society of Friends|Quakers]], Maryland by [[Catholic Church in the United Kingdom|English Catholics]], and Virginia by English [[Anglicans]]. Despite these, and as a result of intervening religious strife and preference in England<ref>See: [[English Civil War]], [[Glorious Revolution]], [[Restoration (England)]], and [[Nonconformist (Protestantism)|Nonconformists]]</ref> the [[Plantation Act 1740]] would set official policy for new immigrants coming to [[British America]] until the [[American Revolution]]. While most settlers and colonists during this time were Protestant, a few early [[Catholic Church|Catholic]] and [[Judaism|Jewish]] settlers also arrived from [[Northwestern Europe]] into the colonies; however, their numbers were very slight compared to the Protestant majority. Even in the "Catholic Proprietary" or colony of Maryland, the vast majority of Maryland colonists were Protestant by 1670.<ref>Richard Middleton, ''Colonial America, A History, 1665-1776'' (Oxford:Blackwell Publishing, 2002), 166-167.</ref>

The text of the [[First Amendment to the United States Constitution|First Amendment]] in the [[United States Constitution|US Constitution]] states that "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof; or abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press; or the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the Government for a redress of grievances."<ref>{{Cite web |title=U.S. Constitution - First Amendment |url=https://constitution.congress.gov/constitution/amendment-1/ |access-date=June 19, 2022 |website=constitution.congress.gov}}</ref> It guarantees the [[Free Exercise Clause of the First Amendment|free exercise]] of religion while also preventing the government from establishing a [[state religion]]. However, the states were not bound by the provision, and as late as the 1830s Massachusetts provided tax money to local Congregational churches.<ref>{{cite book|author=David E. Swift|title=Black Prophets of Justice: Activist Clergy Before the Civil War|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=bUVNttmT13AC&pg=PA180|year=1989|publisher=LSU Press|page=180|isbn=9780807124994|access-date=October 17, 2015|archive-date=April 24, 2016|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160424030433/https://books.google.com/books?id=bUVNttmT13AC&pg=PA180|url-status=live}}</ref> Since the 1940s, the [[Supreme Court of the United States|Supreme Court]] has interpreted the [[Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution|Fourteenth Amendment]] as applying the First Amendment to state and local governments.{{citation needed|date=May 2021}}

[[President of the United States|President]] [[John Adams]] and a unanimous Senate endorsed the [[Treaty of Tripoli]] in 1797 that stated: "the Government of the United States of America is not, in any sense, founded on the Christian religion."<ref>The treaty [https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/bar1796t.asp is online] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190403100415/http://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/bar1796t.asp |date=April 3, 2019 }}</ref>

Eisenhower]], is "[[In God We Trust]]".<ref>{{USC|36|302}} ''National motto''</ref><ref name="USTFS">{{cite web|url=https://www.treasury.gov/about/education/Pages/in-god-we-trust.aspx|title=U.S. on the History of "In God We Trust"|publisher=United States Department of the Treasury|access-date=April 22, 2009|archive-date=April 17, 2016|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160417102334/https://www.treasury.gov/about/education/Pages/in-god-we-trust.aspx|url-status=live}}</ref><ref name="USPL84-851">[https://www.gpo.gov/fdsys/pkg/STATUTE-70/pdf/STATUTE-70-Pg732-2.pdf United States Public Law 84-851] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180705011910/https://www.gpo.gov/fdsys/pkg/STATUTE-70/pdf/STATUTE-70-Pg732-2.pdf |date=July 5, 2018 }}, United States Public Law 84-851.</ref> The phrase first appeared on US coins in 1864.<ref name="USTFS"/>

According to a 2002 survey by the [[Pew Research Center]], nearly 6 in 10 Americans said that religion plays an important role in their lives, compared to 33% in Great Britain, 27% in Italy, 21% in Germany, 12% in Japan, and 11% in France. The survey report stated that the results showed America having a greater similarity to developing nations (where higher percentages say that religion plays an important role) than to other wealthy nations, where religion plays a minor role.<ref name="pewreligion2"/>

Other signs of a decline in religiosity include a decline in the percentage of respondents who say religion is "very important" in their lives compared to those who say it is not (the answer "very important" falling from 70% in 1965 to 45% in 2023, and "not very important" rising from 7 to 28% over the same period in Gallup polls),<ref name="gallup-HWAA-2024">{{cite news |title=How Religious Are Americans? |url=https://news.gallup.com/poll/358364/religious-americans.aspx |access-date=19 August 2024 |work=Gallup |date=29 March 2024}}</ref> and a decline in church attendance (those who report attending church monthly or more often having declined from 52% to 45% from 2007 to 2018, according to a Pew Research Center survey).<ref name="PEW-decline-2019">{{cite web |title=In U.S., Decline of Christianity Continues at Rapid Pace |url=https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2019/10/17/in-u-s-decline-of-christianity-continues-at-rapid-pace/ |website=pewresearch.org |access-date=19 August 2024 |date=17 October 2019}}</ref> Still other sources insist Americans are becoming more religious,<ref name="Johnson 2022" /> and surveys showing otherwise suffer from methodological deficiencies.<ref name="Revisiting Glenn"/><ref name=":13" />

A 2022 study by Pew Research Center estimated that the religiously unaffiliated had reached 30% of the population by 2020. It predicted that in the "most plausible" scenario, accounting for observed acceleration of religious switching, this would increase to 42% by 2050.<ref name=":18">{{Cite web |last=Nadeem |first=Reem |date=2022-09-13 |title=Modeling the Future of Religion in America |url=https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2022/09/13/modeling-the-future-of-religion-in-america/ |access-date=2025-01-29 |website=[[Pew Research Center]] |language=en-US}}</ref>

==Freedom of religion==
[[File:Large Broadside on the Maryland Toleration Act.jpg|thumb|The [[Maryland Toleration Act]] secured religious liberty in the English [[colony of Maryland]]. Similar laws were passed in the [[Rhode Island and Providence Plantations]], [[Connecticut]] and [[Pennsylvania]]. These laws stood in direct contrast with the [[Puritan]] theocratic rule in the [[Plymouth Colony|Plymouth]] and [[Massachusetts Bay Colony|Massachusetts Bay]] colonies.<ref>{{cite web|title=American History: Massachusetts Bay Colony|url=https://www.britannica.com/place/Massachusetts-Bay-Colony|publisher=Encyclopaedia Britannica|access-date=December 15, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201204114232/https://www.britannica.com/place/Massachusetts-Bay-Colony|archive-date=December 4, 2020}}</ref>]]

According to the American legal scholar and academic [[Noah Feldman]], the United States federal government was the first government to be designed with no established religion at all.<ref name=":10">Feldman, Noah (2005). ''Divided by God''. Farrar, Straus and Giroux, p. 10 ("For the first time in recorded history, they designed a government with no established religion at all.")</ref> However, some states had established religions within their borders until the 1830s.

Modeling the provisions concerning religion within the [[Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom]], the framers of the Constitution rejected any religious test for office, and the First Amendment specifically denied the federal government any power to enact any law respecting either an establishment of religion or prohibiting its free exercise, thus protecting any religious organization, institution, or denomination from government interference. The decision was mainly influenced by European Rationalist and Protestant ideals, but was also a consequence of the pragmatic concerns of minority religious groups and small states that did not want to be under the power or influence of a national religion that did not represent them.<ref>Marsden, George M. 1990. ''Religion and American Culture''. Orlando: Harcourt Brace Jovanovich, pp. 45–46.</ref>

===Great Awakenings and other Protestant descendants===
Several Christian groups were founded in America during the [[Great Awakening]]s. Interdenominational [[evangelicalism]] and [[Pentecostalism]] emerged; new Protestant denominations such as [[Adventism]]; non-denominational movements such as the [[Restoration Movement]] (which over time separated into the [[Churches of Christ]], the [[Christian churches and churches of Christ]], and the [[Christian Church (Disciples of Christ)]]); [[Jehovah's Witnesses]] (called "Bible Students" in the latter part of the 19th century); and [[the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints]] ([[Mormonism]]).

===Catholicism===
{{Main|Catholic Church in the United States}}
[[File:The Founding of Maryland.jpg|thumb|''The Founding of Maryland'' (1634) [[artist's impression|depicts]] [[Father Andrew White]], a Jesuit missionary in the left and colonists meeting the people of the Yaocomico branch of the Piscatawy Indian Nation in [[St. Mary's City, Maryland]], the site of Maryland's first colonial settlement.<ref name=archaeology>[https://books.google.com/books?id=nUi479nzegAC&q=leutze ''Archaeology, Narrative, and the Politics of the Past: The View from Southern Maryland''] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200819154603/https://books.google.com/books?id=nUi479nzegAC&vq=leutze&source=gbs_navlinks_s |date=August 19, 2020 }}, p. 52, UPCC book collections on Project MUSE, Julia A. King, Publisher, Univ. of Tennessee Press, 2012, {{ISBN|9781572338883}}</ref>]]
Catholicism first came to the territories now forming the United States by way of [[Spanish colonists]] in the present-day Virgin Islands (1493), Puerto Rico (1508), Florida (1513), South Carolina (1566), Georgia (1568–1684), and the [[southwest United States|southwest]]. The first known Catholic Mass held in what would become the United States was in 1526 by [[Dominican Order|Dominican]] friars [[Antonio de Montesinos (Dominican friar)|Antonio de Montesinos]] and Anthony de Cervantes, who ministered to the [[San Miguel de Gualdape]] colonists for the 3 months the colony existed.<ref>[https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/10534b.htm Schroeder, Henry Joseph. "Antonio Montesino." The Catholic Encyclopedia. Vol. 10. New York: Robert Appleton Company, 1911. 26 Jun. 2014]</ref> The influence of the [[Spanish Missions of California|Alta California missions]] (1769 and onwards) forms a lasting memorial to part of this heritage.  Until the 19th century, the [[Franciscans]] and other religious orders had to operate their missions under the Spanish and [[Portugal|Portuguese]] governments and military.<ref>Franzen, 362</ref>

While the Puritans were securing their Commonwealth, members of the [[Catholic Church]] in England were also planning a refuge, "for they too were being persecuted on account of their religion."<ref name=middle>Richard Middleton, ''Colonial America: a History, 1565-1776'' (Oxford: Blackwell, 2002) 94-101.</ref> Among those interested in providing a refuge for Catholics was the second Lord of Baltimore, George Calvert, who established [[Maryland]], a "Catholic Proprietary", in 1634,<ref name=middle/> more than sixty years after the founding of the Spanish Florida mission of St. Augustine.<ref>Alan Taylor, ''American Colonies'', (New York: Viking, 2001) 76-90.</ref> The first US Catholic university, [[Georgetown University]], was founded in 1789. Though small in number in the beginning, Catholicism grew over the centuries to become the largest single denomination in the United States, primarily through immigration, but also through the acquisition of continental territories under the jurisdiction of French and Spanish Catholic powers.<ref>Taylor, 454-456; 364-395.</ref> Though the European Catholic and indigenous population of these former territories were small,<ref>Taylor, 458-459; 365-366, 368.</ref> the material cultures there, the original mission foundations with their canonical Catholic names, are still recognized today (as they were formerly known) in any number of cities in California, New Mexico and Louisiana. (The most recognizable cities of California, for example, are named after Catholic saints.)
[[File:Basilica of the National Shrine of the Immaculate Conception.jpg|thumb|left|The [[Basilica of the National Shrine of the Immaculate Conception]] in Washington, D.C., is the largest Catholic church in the United States.]]
While Catholic Americans were present in small numbers early in United States history, both in Maryland and in the former French and Spanish colonies that were eventually absorbed into the United States, the vast majority of Catholics in the United States today derive from unprecedented waves of immigration from primarily Catholic countries and regions (Ireland was still part of the United Kingdom until 1921 and German unification didn't officially occur until 1871)<ref>Diarmaid MacCulloch, ''Christianity, The First Three Thousand Years'', (New York: Penguin Books, 2009), 670, 837.</ref> during the mid-to-late 19th and 20th century. [[Irish Americans|Irish]], [[Hispanic American|Hispanic]], [[Italian American|Italian]], [[Portuguese American|Portuguese]], [[French Canadian]], [[Polish American|Polish]], [[German Americans|German]],<ref>''The Catholic Priest in the United States: Historical Investigations'', John Tracey Ellis, editor (Collegeville: St. John's University Press, 1971), 31. Ellis notes: Between 1850-1900,"the net German Catholic population reached a total of 1,134,887, not to mention the additional thousands of German-speaking immigrants during the same years among the 42,289 Catholics from Switzerland, and the 443,230 who came from Austria-Hungry."</ref> [[Lebanese American|Lebanese]] ([[Maronite]]), and [[Syrian Americans|Syrian]] ([[Syriac Catholic Church|Syriac Catholic]]) immigrants largely contributed to the growth in the number of Catholics in the United States. Irish and German Catholics, by far, provided the greatest number of Catholic immigrants before 1900. From 1815 until the close of the Civil War in 1865, 1,683,791 Irish Catholics immigrated to the US. The German states followed, providing "the second largest immigration of Catholics, clergy and lay, some 606,791 in the period 1815-1865, and another 680,000 between 1865 and 1900, while the Irish immigration in the latter period amounted to only 520,000."<ref>Michael V. Gannon, "Before and After Moderism: the Intellectual Isolation of the American Priest," in ''The Catholic Priest in the United States: Historical Investigations'', ed. by John Tracy Ellis (Collegeville: St. John's University Press, 1971), 300.</ref> Of the four major national groups of clergy (early and mid-19th century)—Irish, German, Anglo-American, and French—"the French emigre priests may be said to have been the outstanding men, intellectually."<ref>Gannon, p. 301.</ref> As the number of Catholics increased in the late 19th and 20th century, they built up a vast system of schools (from primary schools to universities) and hospitals. Since then, the Catholic Church has founded hundreds of other colleges and universities, along with thousands of primary and secondary schools. Schools like the [[University of Notre Dame]] is ranked best in its state (Indiana), as Georgetown University is ranked best in the District of Columbia. The following 10 Catholic universities are also ranked among the top 100 universities in the US: University of Notre Dame, Georgetown University, [[Boston College]], [[Santa Clara University]], [[Villanova University]], [[Marquette University]], [[Fordham University]], [[Gonzaga University]], [[Loyola Marymount University]], and the [[University of San Diego]].<ref>''U.S. News & World Report'', College Rankings, 2024.</ref> [[Pope Leo XIV|Leo XIV]] has been [[pope]] since 2025 and is the first pope from the United States.

===Eastern Orthodoxy and Oriental Orthodoxy===
[[Eastern Orthodoxy]] was present in North America since the [[Russian Alaska|Russian colonization of Alaska]]; however, [[Alaska]] would not become a United States territory until 1867, and most Eastern Orthodox Russian settlers in Alaska returned to Russia after the American acquisition of the Alaskan territory. The native converts and a few priests remained behind.

Most Eastern Orthodoxes arrived in the contiguous United States as immigrants beginning in the late 19th century and throughout the 20th century. Two major groups brought Eastern Orthodoxy to America, one were [[Eastern European]]s like [[Russian American|Russians]], [[Greek Americans|Greeks]], [[Ukrainian Americans|Ukrainians]], [[Serbian Americans|Serbians]] and others. The second major group were from [[Levant]] like [[Lebanese American|Lebanese]], [[Syrian American|Syrians]], [[Palestinian American|Palestinians]] and others.

[[Armenian Americans|Armenians]], [[Indian Americans|Indians]], [[Coptic Americans|Copts]] and [[Assyrian Americans|Assyrians]], also brought [[Oriental Orthodoxy]] to America.{{sfn|FitzGerald|2007|p=269-279}}<ref>Alexei D. ===Judaism===
{{Main|American Jews|History of the Jews in the United States}}

After Christianity, [[Judaism]] is the next largest religious affiliation in the United States, though this identification is not necessarily indicative of religious beliefs or practices.<ref name=ARIS2008 /> The Jewish population in the United States was approximately 6 million in 2010.<ref>{{Cite web |url=https://www.jewishdatabank.org/Reports/Jewish_Population_in_the_United_States_2010.pdf |year=2009 |author=Ira M. Jews have been present in what is now the United States since the 17th century and allowed explicitly since the British colonial [[Plantation Act 1740]]. Although small Western European communities initially developed and grew, large-scale immigration did not occur until the late 19th century, mainly due to persecution in parts of [[Eastern Europe]]. The Jewish community in the United States is composed predominantly of [[Ashkenazi Jews]] whose ancestors emigrated from [[Central Europe|Central]] and [[Eastern Europe]]. There are, however, small numbers of older (and some recently arrived) communities of [[Sephardi Jews]] with roots tracing back to 15th-century [[Iberian Peninsula|Iberia]] (Spain, Portugal, and North Africa). There are also [[Mizrahi Jews]] (from the Middle East, [[Caucasus|Caucasia]] and [[Central Asia]]), as well as much smaller numbers of [[Ethiopian Jews]], [[Indian Jews]], and others from various smaller [[Jewish ethnic divisions]]. Approximately 25% of the Jewish American population lives in New York City.<ref>{{cite web |title= Jewish Community Study of New York |publisher= United Jewish Appeal-Federation of New York |year= 2002 |url= https://www.ujafedny.org/atf/cf/%7BAD848866-09C4-482C-9277-51A5D9CD6246%7D/JCommStudyIntro.pdf |access-date= March 22, 2008 |url-status= dead |archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20070614001800/https://www.ujafedny.org/atf/cf/%7BAD848866-09C4-482C-9277-51A5D9CD6246%7D/JCommStudyIntro.pdf |archive-date= June 14, 2007 |df= mdy-all }}</ref>

Among those surveyed, 44% said they were [[Reform Judaism|Reform Jews]], 22% said they were [[Conservative Judaism|Conservative Jews]], and 14% said they were [[Orthodox Judaism|Orthodox Jews]].<ref name="pew2014">{{cite web|url=https://www.pewforum.org/2015/05/12/americas-changing-religious-landscape/|title=America's Changing Religious Landscape|date=May 12, 2015|publisher=[[Pew Research Center]]: Religion & Public Life|access-date=August 19, 2019|archive-date=March 24, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210324055753/https://www.pewforum.org/2015/05/12/americas-changing-religious-landscape/|url-status=live}}</ref><ref name=ciafact>{{cite web |title=CIA Fact Book |url=https://www.cia.gov/the-world-factbook/countries/united-states/ |publisher=CIA World Fact Book |year=2002 |access-date=December 30, 2007 |archive-date=March 21, 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210321202516/https://www.cia.gov/the-world-factbook/countries/united-states/ |url-status=dead }}</ref> According to the 1990 [[National Jewish Population Survey]], 38% of Jews were affiliated with the Reform tradition, 35% were Conservative, 6% were Orthodox, 1% were Reconstructionists, 10% linked themselves to some other tradition, and 10% said they are "just Jewish".<ref>{{cite book |surname=Wertheimer |given=Jack |author-link=Jack Wertheimer |title=Jews in the Center: Conservative Synagogues and Their Members|url={{Google books|id=U_nEoAZ6ffgC|plainurl=y|page=68|keywords=|text=}} |year=2002 |publisher=Rutgers University Press |page=68 |isbn=9780813532066 |archive-date=January 9, 2016 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160109094436/https://books.google.com/books?id=U_nEoAZ6ffgC&pg=PA68 |url-status=live}}</ref>

Thus, the majority of American Jews affiliated themselves with the major Jewish movements: Conservative, Orthodox and Reform Judaism.<ref name="Wertheimer1991">{{cite book |surname=Wertheimer |given=Jack |author-link=Jack Wertheimer |chapter=Recent Trends in American Judaism |editor-surname=Neusner |editor-given=Jacob |editor-link=Jacob Neusner |title=An Introduction to Judaism: A Textbook and Reader |place=Louisville, Kentucky |publisher=Westminster/John Knox Press |year=1991 |pages=85–116 |chapter-url={{Google books|id=6M8oxDql1KIC|plainurl=y|page=85|keywords=|text=}} |url={{Google books|id=6M8oxDql1KIC|plainurl=y}} |isbn=0-664-25348-2}} pp. 86–88.</ref><ref>{{cite book |surname=Wertheimer |given=Jack |author-link=Jack Wertheimer |title=The New American Judaism: How Jews Practice Their Religion Today |place=Princeton, NJ; Oxford |publisher=Princeton University Press |year=2018 |url={{Google books|id=1DthDwAAQBAJ|plainurl=y|page=103|keywords=|text=}} |page=103 |isbn=978-0-691-18129-5}}</ref> Already in the 1980s, 20–30% of members of the largest Jewish communities, such as of New York City, Chicago, and Miami, rejected a [[Jewish religious movements|denominational]] label.<ref name="Wertheimer1991" />

According to the 2001 [[National Jewish Population Survey]], 4.3&nbsp;million [[American Jewish]] adults have some sort of strong connection to the Jewish community, whether religious or cultural.<ref name=NJPS>{{cite web | url=https://www.ujc.org/content_display.html?ArticleID=83784 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20040515195547/https://www.ujc.org/content_display.html?ArticleID=83784 | url-status=dead | archive-date=May 15, 2004 | title=2001 National Jewish Population Survey | publisher=Ujc.org | access-date=December 29, 2012 | df=mdy-all }}</ref> [[Jewish identity|Jewishness]] is generally considered an [[ethnic]] identity as well as a [[religious]] one. The Jewish American community has higher household incomes than average and is one of the best-educated religious communities in the United States.<ref name=pew15 />

Out of all religious groups surveyed by ISPU, Muslims were found to be the most likely to report experiences of religious discrimination (61%). That can also be broken down when looking at gender (with Muslim women more likely than Muslim men to experience racial discrimination), age (with young people more likely to report experiencing racial discrimination than older people), and race (with Arab Muslims the most likely to report experiencing religious discrimination). Muslims born in the United States are more likely to experience all three forms of discrimination: gender, religious, and racial.<ref name=":2" />

Research indicates that Muslims in the United States are generally more assimilated and prosperous than their counterparts in Europe.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.projectmaps.com/AMP2004report.pdf |title=Zogby phone survey |publisher=Projectmaps.com |access-date=March 17, 2012 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130626110709/https://www.projectmaps.com/AMP2004report.pdf |archive-date=June 26, 2013 }}</ref><ref>{{cite web |url= https://www.voanews.com/a/a-13-muslims2006-09-10-voa17/320857.html |title= America's Muslims after 9/11 |publisher= Voice of America |access-date= May 22, 2010 |archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20110717231216/https://www.voanews.com/english/news/a-13-Muslims2006-09-10-voa17.html |archive-date= July 17, 2011 |url-status= live }}</ref><ref name="Muslim Americans">{{cite web |url=https://pewresearch.org/assets/pdf/muslim-americans.pdf |title=Muslim Americans, Pew Research Center |access-date=December 29, 2012 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120916051122/https://pewresearch.org/assets/pdf/muslim-americans.pdf |archive-date=September 16, 2012 }}</ref> Like other subcultural and religious communities, the Islamic community has generated its own political organizations and charity organizations.

===Buddhism===
{{Main|Buddhism in the United States}}
[[File:Hongwanji Buddist Temple.jpg|thumb|left|upright=.9|Services at the Hompa Hongwanji Buddhist Temple, [[Los Angeles]], around 1925]]

[[Buddhism]] entered the United States during the 19th century with the arrival of the first immigrants from [[East Asia]]. The first Buddhist temple was established in [[San Francisco]] in 1853 by [[Chinese Americans]]. The first prominent US citizen to publicly convert to Buddhism was Colonel [[Henry Steel Olcott]] in 1880, who is still honored in [[Sri Lanka]] for his Buddhist revival efforts. An event that contributed to the strengthening of Buddhism in the United States was the [[Parliament of the World's Religions]] in 1893, which was attended by many Buddhist delegates sent from India, China, Japan, [[Vietnam]], [[Thailand]] and [[Sri Lanka]].

In the late 19th century, Buddhist missionaries from [[Japan]] traveled to the US, and during the same time period, US intellectuals started to take an interest in Buddhism.

The early 20th century was characterized by continuing tendencies rooted in the 19th century. The second half, by contrast, saw the emergence of new approaches and the move of Buddhism into the mainstream, making itself a mass and social-religious phenomenon.<ref>{{cite web| url=https://www.utne.com/Mind-Body/Buddhism-Fastest-Growing-American-Religion-Stigma.aspx| title=Bad Buddhist Vibes| work=Utne| date=December 20, 2011| access-date=March 5, 2015| archive-date=May 12, 2013| archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130512155622/http://www.utne.com/Mind-Body/Buddhism-Fastest-Growing-American-Religion-Stigma.aspx| url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{cite book |url=https://www.ucpress.edu/book.php?isbn=9780520213012 |title=The Faces of Buddhism in America |series=[[University of California Press]] |access-date=March 5, 2015 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210225014437/https://www.ucpress.edu/book.php?isbn=9780520213012 |archive-date=February 25, 2021 |url-status=live}}</ref>

According to a 2016 study, Buddhists are approximately 1% of the American population.<ref name=AVA2017/> According to the Association of Statisticians of American Religious Bodies newsletter published in March 2017, based on data from 2010, Buddhists were the largest minority religion in 186 counties out of the 3143 counties in the country.<ref name=ASARB-March2017/>

===Hinduism===

[[File:Iraivan Temple.jpg|thumb|left|[[Saiva Siddhanta Temple]] on [[Kauaʻi Island]] in Hawaii is the only Hindu monastery in the United States]]

[[Hinduism]] represents approximately 1% of the U.S. population in 2010s.<ref name="AVA2017" /><ref name="first_american_hindu_census">[http://66.223.50.234/asrec/archive/papers/Melton_Hindu_Demographics.pdf ''Reflections on Hindu Demographics in America: An Initial Report on the First American Hindu Census.''] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191020183913/http://66.223.50.234/asrec/archive/papers/Melton_Hindu_Demographics.pdf|date=October 20, 2019}} [[J. The first time [[Hinduism]] entered the United States is not clearly identifiable. However, large groups of Hindus have immigrated from [[India]], [[Sri Lanka]], [[Nepal]], [[Pakistan]], [[Bangladesh]], [[Guyana]], [[Trinidad and Tobago]], other parts of the [[Caribbean]], [[southern Africa]], [[eastern Africa]], [[Singapore]], [[Malaysia]], [[Indonesia]], [[Mauritius]], [[Fiji]], [[Europe]], [[Australia]], [[New Zealand]], and other regions and countries since the enactment of the [[Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965]]. During the same decades, the [[International Society for Krishna Consciousness]] (ISKCON), a [[Vaishnavism|Vaishnavite]] [[Hindu reform movements|Hindu reform]] organization, was founded in the US by [[A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada]]. In 2003, the [[Hindu American Foundation]]—a national institution protecting the rights of the Hindu community of the US—was founded.

According to the Association of Statisticians of American Religious Bodies newsletter published in March 2017, based on data from 2010, Hindus were the largest minority religion in 92 counties out of the 3143 counties in the country.<ref name="ASARB-March2017" />

American Hindus have one of the highest rates of educational attainment and household income among all religious communities and tend to have lower divorce rates.<ref name="pew15" /> Hindus also have higher acceptance towards [[homosexuality]] (71%), which is higher than the general public (62%).<ref>{{cite news |title=Pewforum religious landscape study |url=https://www.pewforum.org/religious-landscape-study/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200103045246/https://www.pewforum.org/religious-landscape-study/ |archive-date=January 3, 2020 |access-date=February 20, 2020 |newspaper=Pew Research Center's Religion & Public Life Project}}</ref>

=== Native American religions ===
{{Main|Native American religions}}
{{See also|Mythologies of the indigenous peoples of North America}}
[[File:Bear Butte Scan 0001.jpg|thumb|[[Bear Butte]], in South Dakota, is a sacred site for over 30 [[Plains tribes]].]]
[[File:Fort_Hall_Reservation._Shoshone_Indian_Sun_Dance_-_NARA_-_298649.tif|thumb|[[Shoshone]] [[Sun Dance]] at [[Fort Hall]], 1925.]]
Native American [[ethnic religion|ethnic]] and [[indigenous religion|indigenous faiths]] historically exhibited much diversity, and are often characterized by [[animism]] or [[panentheism]] and [[shamanism]].{{sfnm|1a1=Underhill|1y=1965|1pp=|2a1=Gill|2y=1982|2pp=|3a1=Hultkrantz|3y=1987|3pp=|4a1=Hirschfelder|4a2=Molin|4y=2000|4pp=|5a1=Garrett|5a2=Garrett|5y=2003|5pp=|6a1=Melton|6y=2009|6loc="Native American Religions"|6pp=|7a1=Utter|7y=2001|7p=145|8a1=Paper|8y=2007|8pp=}}<ref name="Leavelle">{{cite book |surname=Leavelle |given=Tracy Neal |chapter=American Indians |title=The Blackwell Companion to Religion in America |editor-surname=Goff |editor-given=Philip |year=2010 |pages=397–416 |place=Malden, Ma; Oxford |publisher=Wiley-Blackwell |chapter-url={{Google books|id=ZohdcPSo3LsC|plainurl=y|page=397}} |url={{Google books|id=ZohdcPSo3LsC|plainurl=y}} |isbn=978-1-4051-6936-3}}</ref> Common concept is the supernatural world of [[deity|deities]], spirits and wonders, such as the [[Algonquian peoples|Algonquian]] ''[[manitou]]'' or the [[Lakota people|Lakota]]'s ''[[Wakan Tanka|wakan]]''.{{sfn|Underhill|1965|pp=1–9}}{{sfn|Hultkrantz|1987|p=}} In most areas, without Christian influence, was known a supreme [[Great Spirit]] or [[sky deity]].{{sfn|Hultkrantz|1987|p=}} Their great [[creation myth]]s and sacred [[oral tradition]] in whole, as anthropologists note, are comparable to the Christian Bible.{{sfn|Tooker|1979|p=31}}

The membership of Native American religions in the 21st century comprises about 9,000 people.<ref>Or about .003% of the US population of 300 million.{{cite book| author=James T. Richardson| title=Regulating Religion: Case Studies from Around the Globe| url={{Google books|id=U6opyVE_IYkC|plainurl=y|page=543}}| year=2004| publisher=Springer| page=543| isbn=9780306478864| archive-date=2016-04-24| archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160424030516/https://books.google.com/books?id=U6opyVE_IYkC&pg=PA543| url-status=live}}</ref> Since Native Americans practicing traditional ceremonies do not usually have public organizations or membership rolls, these "members" estimates are likely substantially lower than the actual numbers of people who participate in traditional ceremonies.{{sfn|Tooker|1979|p=}}

The following is a list of Indigenous American religions those still survive to some degree at the beginning of the 21st century:{{sfnm|1a1=Underhill|1y=1965|1pp=|2a1=Brown|2y=1982|2pp=1–28|3a1=Brown|3a2=Cousins|3y=2001|3pp=|4a1=Hirschfelder|4a2=Molin|4y=2000|4pp=|5a1=Garrett|5a2=Garrett|5y=2003|5pp=|6a1=Melton|6y=2009|6loc="Native American Religions"|6pp=}}<ref name="Leavelle" /> [[Alaska Native religion]]s, [[Abenaki mythology|Abenaki]], [[Anishinaabe traditional beliefs|Anishinaabe]] ([[Ojibwe#Spiritual beliefs|Ojibwe]], [[Midewiwin]] society), [[Apache#Religion|Apache]], [[Blackfoot mythology|Blackfoot]], [[Traditional narratives of Indigenous Californians|Californian]] ([[Kuksu (religion)|Kuksu religion]], [[Miwok mythology|Miwok]], [[Ohlone mythology|Ohlone]] and [[Pomo religion|Pomo]]), [[Choctaw mythology|Choctaw]], [[Crow religion|Crow]], [[Haida mythology|Haida]], [[Ho-Chunk mythology|Ho-Chunk]], [[Iroquois mythology|Iroquois]] ([[Cherokee spiritual beliefs|Cherokee]], [[Mohawk people#Religion|Mohawk]], [[Creek mythology|Muscogee Creek]], [[Seneca mythology|Seneca]] and [[Wyandot religion|Wyandot]]), [[Jivaroan peoples#Religion|Jivaroan]], [[Kwakwakaʼwakw mythology|Kwakwakaʼwakw]], [[Lenape mythology|Lenape]], [[Mapuche religion|Mapuche]], [[Navajo#Spiritual and religious beliefs|Navajo]], [[Nuu-chah-nulth mythology|Nuu-chah-nulth]], [[Pawnee mythology|Pawnee]], [[Pueblo religion|Pueblo]] ([[Acoma Pueblo#Religion|Acoma Pueblo]], [[Hopi mythology|Hopi]] and [[Zuni mythology|Zuni]]), [[Sioux#Religion|Sioux]] ([[Assiniboine#Mythology|Assiniboine]], Dakota and [[Lakota religion|Lakota]]), [[Tsimshian mythology|Tsimshian]], [[Ute mythology|Ute]], and [[Yaqui#Yaqui cosmology and religion|Yaqui]] beliefs.

There are also numerous [[indigenism|indigenist]] [[revitalization movement]]s within them that are divided into fundamentalist and reform.{{sfn|Underhill|1965|pp=254–270}}{{sfn|Champagne|2005|p=}}

Generally fundamentalist movements include the [[Pueblo Revolt]] (1680s), the [[Shawnee]] Prophet movement (1805–1811), the [[Cherokee]] Prophet movement (1811–1813), the [[Creek War|Red Stick War]] (1813–1814), White Path's Rebellion (1826), the [[Ho-Chunk|Winnebago]] Prophet movement (1830–1832), the first Ghost Dance (1869–1870) and the second [[Ghost Dance]] (1889–1890), and the Snake movements among the Cherokee, [[Choctaw]], and [[Muscogee|Muscogee Creek]] peoples during the 1890s.{{sfn|Champagne|2005|p=}}

Generally [[religious syncretism|syncretic]] reform movements include the Yaqui religion (1500–present), the [[Longhouse Religion|Longhouse religion]] (1797–present), the Munsee Prophetess movement (1804–1805), the Kickapoo Prophet movement (1815–present), the [[Keetoowah Nighthawk Society|Cherokee Keetoowah Society]] (1858–present), the [[Native American religions#Washat Dreamers Religion|Washat Dreamers religion]] (1850–present), the [[Indian Shaker Church|Indian Shakers]] (1881–present), the [[Native American Church]] (1800s–present), the Shoshoni [[Sun Dance]] (1890–present), the New Tidings religion or ''[[Wocekiye]]'' of the Canadian [[Sioux]] (1900–present), and Ojibwe Drummer movement (contemporary).{{sfn|Champagne|2005|p=}}

Thus, the [[Longhouse Religion]] combines and reinterprets elements of traditional [[Iroquois]] beliefs with a revised code such as must refrain from drinking, selling off land, intensive animal farming, and witchcraft, meant to revive traditional consciousness after a long period of cultural disintegration following colonization. It was founded in 1797 by the [[Seneca people|Seneca]] prophet [[Handsome Lake]] (Sganyodaiyoˀ). The movement had about 5,000 practicing members as of 1969.{{sfn|Wallace|2010|p=3}}

Since 1889, in accordance with the [[Millenarianism|millenarian]] teachings of the [[Northern Paiute]] spiritual leader [[Wovoka]], the [[Ghost Dance]] ceremony was incorporated into numerous native belief systems.{{sfn|Mooney|1896}}{{sfn|Kehoe|2006}}

The [[Sun Dance]] is a prominent living ceremony and movement, the [[Shoshone]] by origin in 1890, practiced by a number of peoples, primarily those of the [[Plains Indians|Plains Nations]]. Many of the ceremonies have features in common, such as specific dances and songs, the use of drums, the [[ceremonial pipe]], praying, fasting, and, in some cases, the piercing of the skin as a sacrifice. At most ceremonies, other participants stay in the surrounding camp and pray to support the dancers.{{sfn|Champagne|2005|p=}}{{sfn|Underhill|1965|pp=142–153}}

The [[Native American Church]] is a 19th-century origin syncretistic religious tradition involving the ceremonial and sacred use of ''[[Lophophora williamsii]]'' ([[peyote]]).{{sfn|Aberle|1991|pp=}}{{sfn|Stewart|1987|pp=}}<ref>{{cite web |last=Beyer |first=Catherine |date=2015-04-02 |title=Peyote and the Native American Church |access-date=2023-12-04 |url=http://altreligion.about.com/od/alternativereligionsaz/p/Peyote-And-The-Native-American-Church.htm|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150402092953/http://altreligion.about.com/od/alternativereligionsaz/p/Peyote-And-The-Native-American-Church.htm |archive-date=April 2, 2015 }}</ref><ref>{{cite web |last=Fikes |first=Jay |date=2007-08-21 |title=CSP — 'A Brief History of the Native American Church' |url=http://www.csp.org/communities/docs/fikes-nac_history.html |access-date=2023-12-04 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20070821191748/http://www.csp.org/communities/docs/fikes-nac_history.html |archive-date=August 21, 2007 }}</ref>

===Neopaganism===
{{Main|Contemporary Paganism}}
Neopaganism in the United States is represented by widely different [[List of Neopagan movements|movements and organizations]]. The largest Neopagan religion is [[Wicca]], followed by [[Neo-Druidism]].<ref>Barbara Jane Davy, ''Introduction to Pagan Studies'', p. 151 (2007)</ref><ref>Rosemary Guiley, ''The Encyclopedia of Magic and Alchemy'', p. 84 (2006)</ref> Other neopagan movements include [[Germanic Neopaganism]], [[Celtic Reconstructionist Paganism]], [[Hellenic Polytheistic Reconstructionism]], and [[Semitic neopaganism]].

====Wicca====
Wicca advanced in North America in the 1960s by [[Raymond Buckland]], an expatriate Briton who visited Gardner's Isle of Man coven to gain initiation.<ref>Raymond Buckland, ''Scottish Witchcraft: The history & magick of the Picts'', p. 246 (1991)</ref> [[Universal Eclectic Wicca]] was popularized in 1969 for a diverse membership drawing from both [[Dianic Wicca|Dianic]] and [[British Traditional Wicca|British Traditional]] Wiccan backgrounds.<ref>{{cite web |title=Siver Chalice Ancestry |url=https://www.angelfire.com/rant/ingwitch/sca.html |website=[[Angelfire]] |access-date=February 16, 2022 |archive-date=February 24, 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210224153506/https://www.angelfire.com/rant/ingwitch/sca.html |url-status=live }}</ref>

====Druidry====
According to the [[Institute for the Study of Secularism in Society and Culture#American Religious Identification Survey (ARIS)|American Religious Identification Survey]] (ARIS), there are approximately 30,000 [[druid]]s in the United States.<ref>Trinity ARIS 2008; [https://prog.trincoll.edu/ISSSC/DataArchive/Access.asp Trinity ARIS 2001] {{webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100625210111/https://prog.trincoll.edu/ISSSC/DataArchive/Access.asp |date=June 25, 2010 }}</ref> Modern Druidism arrived in North America first in the form of fraternal Druidic organizations in the nineteenth century, and orders such as the Ancient Order of Druids in America were founded as distinct American groups as early as 1912. In 1963, the [[Reformed Druids of North America]] (RDNA) was established by students at [[Carleton College]], [[Northfield, Minnesota]]. They adopted elements of Neopaganism into their practices, for instance, celebrating the festivals of the [[Wheel of the Year]].<ref>[[#Adl06|Adler 2006]]. pp. 337–339.</ref>

===New Thought Movement===
{{Main|New Thought}}
A group of churches that started in the 1830s in the United States is known under the banner of "[[New Thought]]." These churches share a [[Spirituality|spiritual]], [[metaphysics|metaphysical]] and [[mysticism|mystical]] predisposition and understanding of the [[Bible]] and were strongly influenced by the [[Transcendentalist]] movement, particularly the work of [[Ralph Waldo Emerson]]. Another antecedent of this movement was [[Swedenborgianism]], founded on the writings of [[Emanuel Swedenborg]] in 1787.<ref>William James, {{Deprecated archive|url=https://archive.today/20120707202152/https://etext.virginia.edu/etcbin/toccer-new2?id=JamVari.sgm&images=images/modeg&data=/texts/english/modeng/parsed&tag=parsed&tag=public&part=all|title="The Varieties of Religious Experience"}}. pp. 92–93. New York 1929</ref> The New Thought concept was named by [[Emma Curtis Hopkins]] ("teacher of teachers") after Hopkins broke off from [[Mary Baker Eddy]]'s [[Church of Christ, Scientist]]. The movement had been previously known as the Mental Sciences or the Christian Sciences. The three major branches are [[Religious Science]], [[Unity Church]], and [[Divine Science]].

* A late 2009 online Harris poll of 2,303 US adults (18 and older)<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.harrisinteractive.com/vault/Harris_Poll_2009_12_15.pdf|title=What People Do and Do Not Believe in|date=December 15, 2009|work=Harris Interactive|access-date=May 15, 2011|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110124052812/https://www.harrisinteractive.com/vault/Harris_Poll_2009_12_15.pdf|archive-date=January 24, 2011|url-status=dead}}</ref> found that "82% of adult Americans believe in God", the same number as in two earlier polls in 2005 and 2007. Another 9% said they did not believe in God, and 9% said that they were not sure. It further concluded, "Large majorities also believe in miracles (76%), heaven (75%), that Jesus is God or the Son of God (73%), in angels (72%), the survival of the soul after death (71%), and in the resurrection of Jesus (70%). Less than half (45%) of adults believe in Darwin's theory of [[evolution]] but this is more than the 40% who believe in [[creationism]]..... Many people consider themselves Christians without necessarily believing in some of the key beliefs of Christianity. However, this is not true of [[born-again Christian]]s. In addition to their religious beliefs, large minorities of adults, including many Christians, have "[[pagan]]" or pre-Christian beliefs such as a belief in ghosts, [[astrology]], witches and [[reincarnation]].... Because the sample is based on those who agreed to participate in the Harris Interactive panel, no estimates of theoretical sampling error can be calculated."
* A 2008 survey of 1,000 people concluded that, based on their stated beliefs rather than their religious identification, 69.5% of Americans believe in a personal God, roughly 12.3% of Americans are [[atheist]] or [[agnostic]], and another 12.1% are deistic (believing in a higher power/non-personal God, but no personal God).<ref name="ARIS2008" />
* Mark Chaves, a [[Duke University]] professor of sociology, religion and divinity, found that 92% of Americans believed in God in 2008, but that significantly fewer Americans have great confidence in their religious leaders than a generation ago.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://today.duke.edu/2011/08/chaves|title=according to Mark Chaves|date=August 16, 2011|publisher=Today.duke.edu|author=Eric Ferreri|access-date=December 29, 2012|archive-date=January 27, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210127104803/https://today.duke.edu/2011/08/chaves|url-status=live}}</ref>
* According to a 2008 ARIS survey, belief in God varies considerably by region. The lowest rate is in the West with 59% reporting a belief in God, and the highest rate is in the South at 86%.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.gallup.com/poll/109108/Belief-God-Far-Lower-Western-US.aspx|title=Belief in God Far Lower in Western U.S.|date=July 28, 2008|publisher=[[The Gallup Organization]]|author=Newport, Frank|access-date=September 4, 2010|archive-date=September 11, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170911051359/http://www.gallup.com/poll/109108/Belief-God-Far-Lower-Western-US.aspx|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Spiritual but not religious===
{{main|Spiritual but not religious}}

"Spiritual but not religious" (SBNR) is self-identified stance of spirituality that takes issue with organized religion as the sole or most valuable means of furthering spiritual growth. Spirituality places an emphasis upon the wellbeing of the "mind-body-spirit",<ref>Heelas, Spiritualities of Life, 63.</ref> so holistic activities such as tai chi, reiki, and yoga are common within the SBNR movement.<ref>Heelas, Spiritualities of Life, 64.</ref> In contrast to religion, spirituality has often been associated with the interior life of the individual.<ref>Carette and King, Selling Spirituality, 41.</ref>

One fifth of the US public and a third of adults under the age of 30 are reportedly unaffiliated with any religion, however they identify as being spiritual in some way. Of these religiously unaffiliated Americans, 37% classify themselves as spiritual but not religious.<ref>{{cite web|last1=Funk|first1=Cary|last2=Smith|first2=Greg|title="Nones" on the Rise: One-in-Five Adults Have No Religious Affiliation|url=https://www.pewforum.org/files/2012/10/NonesOnTheRise-full.pdf|website=pewforum.org|publisher=The Pew Forum on Religion and Public Life|access-date=December 6, 2015|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140826234925/http://www.pewforum.org/files/2012/10/NonesOnTheRise-full.pdf|archive-date=August 26, 2014|url-status=dead}}</ref>

According to some sociologists, perceptions of religious decline are a popular misconception.<ref name="Johnson 2022" /> They state that surveys showing so suffer from methodological deficiencies, that Americans are becoming more religious, and that [[atheism|Atheists]] and [[Agnosticism|Agnostics]] make up a small and stable percentage of the population.<ref name="Revisiting Glenn">{{cite journal |last1=Johnson |first1=Byron |last2=Stark |first2=Rodney |last3=Bradshaw |first3=Matt |last4=Levin |first4=Jeff |date=2022 |title=Are Religious "Nones" Really Not Religious?: Revisiting Glenn, Three Decades Later |journal=Interdisciplinary Journal of Research on Religion |volume=18 |issue=7 |quote=As briefly noted above, proportions of atheists and intense, practicing Christians appear to be somewhat stable across time, casting some doubt on a major decline in religiosity (Stark 2008, 2011; Hout and Smith 2015; Stetzer 2015). According to Stark (2008:177), data from multiple population surveys show that the proportion of the U.S. population that identifies as atheist was unchanged for at least 70 years, from the 1940s until the past decade, at about 4%.}}</ref><ref name=":7">{{Cite news |last1=Johnson |first1=Byron R. |last2=Levin |first2=Jeff |date=2022-07-28 |title=Religion Is Dying? Don't Believe It |url=https://www.wsj.com/articles/religion-is-dying-dont-believe-it-nones-others-surveys-faith-institutions-atheists-agnostics-practice-minority-11659017037 |access-date=2023-09-20 |work=[[The Wall Street Journal]] |language=en-US |issn=0099-9660}}</ref> "Religious belief and interest" has remained relatively stable in recent years; "organizational participation", in contrast, has decreased.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Grose |first=Jessica |date=April 19, 2023 |title=Lots of Americans Are Losing Their Religion. Have You? |url=https://www.nytimes.com/2023/04/19/opinion/religion-america.html |access-date=October 23, 2023 |website=[[The New York Times]]}}</ref>

==Major US-origin movements==

===Christian===

* [[Adventism]] — began as an inter-denominational movement during the [[Second Great Awakening]] (1790–1840). Its most vocal leader was [[William Miller (preacher)|William Miller]], who in the 1830s in [[New York (state)|New York]] became convinced of an imminent [[Second Coming]] of Jesus. The most prominent modern group to emerge from this is the [[Seventh-day Adventists]].<ref>{{cite book |surname=Olson |given=Roger E. |author-link=Roger E. Since 1960, it has affected most denominations in the US, and has spread widely across the world.{{sfn|Reid|Linder|Shelley|Stout|1990|pp=241–242}}

[[Mormonism]], founded in 1830 by [[Joseph Smith]] in [[upstate New York]] — a product of the [[Christian revival#United States 1800–1850|Christian revivalist]] movement of the Second Great Awakening and based in [[Christian primitivism]]. Multiple [[List of sects in the Latter Day Saint movement|Latter Day Saint denominations]] can be found throughout the United States. [[The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints]] (LDS Church), the largest denomination, is headquartered in [[Salt Lake City]], [[Utah]], while The [[Community of Christ]] (also known as the Reorganised Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints), the second-largest denomination, is headquartered in [[Independence, Missouri]].{{sfn|Backman|1983|loc=}}{{sfn|Mauss|1994}}{{sfn|Bowman|2012}}

* [[Pentecostalism]] — a large movement which emphasizes the role of the [[baptism with the Holy Spirit]] and the use of [[spiritual gift]]s, finds its historic roots in the [[Azusa Street Revival]] in Los Angeles from 1904 to 1906, sparked by [[Charles Fox Parham|Charles Parham]].{{sfn|Backman|1983|loc=}}{{sfn|Ahlstrom|2004|pp=1103–6}}{{sfn|Robeck|2006}}

* [[Restoration Movement]], a.k.a. [[Campbellite|Campbellism]] — groups that began during the Second Great Awakening (1790–1840) and idealized early Christianity.{{sfn|Backman|1983|loc=}}{{sfn|Garrett|2002|p=}}
* [[Universalist Church of America]]'s first regional conference was founded in 1793.{{sfn|Eddy|1884}}

The [[United States census|US census]] does not ask about religion. Various groups have conducted surveys to determine approximate percentages of those affiliated with each religious group.

===Census and independent polling===
Since the first [[1790 United States census|American census in 1790]], census forms have never asked the religion of participants, with [[Vincent Barabba|Vincent P. Barabba]], former head of the [[United States Census Bureau]], stating in April 1976 that "asking such a question in the decennial census, in which replies are mandatory, would appear to infringe upon the traditional [[separation of church and state]]" and "could affect public cooperation in the census". Data on religious affiliation comes from [[Opinion poll|independent pollsters]]<ref>{{Cite web |last1=Diamant |first1=Jeff |last2=Leppert |first2=Rebecca |date=April 12, 2023 |title=Why the U.S. census doesn't ask Americans about their religion |url=https://www.pewresearch.org/fact-tank/2023/04/12/why-the-us-census-doesnt-ask-americans-about-their-religion/ |access-date=2023-04-14 |website=Pew Research Center |quote=Census forms in the United States don't ask about religion, but relatively few U.S. adults (25%) know this, according to a Pew Research Center survey conducted shortly before census forms were mailed out in 2020. Indeed, while the Census Bureau has long collected troves of data on Americans' income, employment, race, ethnicity, housing and other things, the decennial census, held since 1790, has never directly asked Americans about their religion.}}</ref> by the [[Pew Research Center]] and other agencies or, on membership, from religious associations, such as the ''[[Yearbook of American and Canadian Churches]]'' of the [[National Council of Churches]].

===Problems measuring religion===
====Inaccuracies of independent polling====
Independent polling results on religion are questionable due to numerous factors:<ref>{{cite book|title=Inventing American Religion: Polls Surveys, and the Tenuous Quest for a Nation's Faith|last1=Wuthnow|first1=Robert|publisher=Oxford University Press|year=2015|isbn=9780190258900|chapter=8. Taking Stock}}</ref>
* polls consistently fail to predict political election outcomes, signifying consistent failure to capture the actual views of the population
* very low response rates for all polls since the 1990s
* biases in wording or topic affect how people respond to polls
* polls categorize people based on limited choices
* polls often generalize broadly
* polls have shallow or superficial choices, which complicate capturing complexity of religious beliefs and practices
* poll interviewer and respondent fatigue is very common

Researchers note that an estimated 20-40% of the population changes their self-reported religious affiliation/identity over time due to numerous factors and that usually it is their answers on surveys that change, not necessarily their religious practices or beliefs.<ref name="Johnson 2022">{{cite journal |last1=Johnson |first1=Byron |last2=Stark |first2=Rodney |last3=Bradshaw |first3=Matt |last4=Levin |first4=Jeff |title=Are Religious "Nones" Really Not Religious?: Revisiting Glenn, Three Decades Later |journal=Interdisciplinary Journal of Research on Religion |date=2022 |volume=18 |issue=7}}</ref>

Researchers advise caution when looking at the "Nones" demographics on surveys because different surveys systematically have discrepancies that amount to 8% and growing of estimates, part of it being that the respondents on surveys are not consistent and also the questions asked are worded differently, generating consistent discrepancies in responses.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Burge |first1=Ryan P. |title=How Many "Nones" Are There? Explaining the Discrepancies in Survey Estimates |journal=Review of Religious Research |date=March 2020 |volume=62 |issue=1 |pages=173–190 |doi=10.1007/s13644-020-00400-7|s2cid=256240351 }}</ref>

According to Gallup there are variations on the responses based on how they ask questions. They routinely ask on complex things like belief in God since the early 2000s in 3 different wordings and they constantly receive 3 different percentages in responses.<ref>{{cite web |last1=Saad |first1=Lydia |last2=Hrynowski |first2=Zach |title=How Many Americans Believe in God? |url=https://news.gallup.com/poll/268205/americans-believe-god.aspx |website=Gallup.com |publisher=Gallup |language=en |date=24 June 2022 |quote=The answer to how many Americans believe in God depends on how the question is asked. Gallup has measured U.S. adults' belief in God three different ways in recent years, with varying results.}}</ref>

===Public Religion Research Institute data (2020)===

The [[Public Religion Research Institute]] (PRRI) has made annual estimates about religious adherence in the United States every year since 2013, and they most recently updated their data in 2020. Their data can be broken down to the state level, and data has also been made available of several large metro areas. Data is collected from roughly 50,000 telephone interviews conducted every year.<ref name="PRRI">{{cite web |title=PRRI — American Values Atlas |url=https://ava.prri.org |website=The American Values Atlas |publisher=Public Religion Research Institute |access-date=July 9, 2021 |archive-date=February 11, 2022 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220211132716/http://ava.prri.org/ |url-status=live }}</ref>

Their most recent data shows that approximately 70% of Americans are Christians (down from 71% in 2013), with about 46% of the population professing belief in [[Protestant Christianity]], and another 22% adhering to [[Catholicism]]. About 23% of the population adheres to no religion, and 7% more of the population professes a Non-Christian religion (such as [[Judaism]], [[Islam]], or [[Hinduism]]).<ref name="PRRI" /><ref>{{Cite web |title=The 2020 Census of American Religion |url=https://www.prri.org/research/2020-census-of-american-religion/ |access-date=2023-01-18 |website=PRRI |date=July 8, 2021 |language=en-US}}</ref>

Adjustments were made for those congregations that did not respond and for religious groups that reported only adult membership.<ref>{{cite web |url= https://www.thearda.com/mapsReports/rcms_notes.asp |title= ARDA Sources for Religious Congregations & Membership Data |publisher= [[Association of Religion Data Archives|ARDA]] |year= 2000 |access-date= May 29, 2010 |archive-date= July 25, 2012 |archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20120725042715/http://www.thearda.com/mapsreports/RCMS_Notes.asp |url-status= live }}</ref> ASARB estimates that most of the churches not responding were black Protestant congregations. ===ARIS findings regarding self-identification===<!---[[American Religious Identification Survey]] redirects here --->
The United States government does not collect religious data in its census. The survey below, the [[Institute for the Study of Secularism in Society and Culture#American Religious Identification Survey (ARIS)|American Religious Identification Survey]] (ARIS) of 2008, was a random digit-dialed telephone [[statistical survey|survey]] of 54,461 American residential households in the [[contiguous United States]]. The 1990 sample size was 113,723; 2001 sample size was 50,281.

Adult respondents were asked the [[open-ended question]], "What is your religion, if any?" Interviewers did not prompt or offer a suggested list of potential answers. The religion of the spouse or partner was also asked. If the initial answer was "Protestant" or "Christian" further questions were asked to probe which particular denomination. About one third of the sample was asked more detailed demographic questions.

'''Highlights:'''<ref name=ARIS2008 />
# The ARIS 2008 survey was carried out during February–November 2008 and collected answers from 54,461 respondents who were questioned in English or Spanish.
#The American population self-identifies as predominantly Christian, but Americans are slowly becoming less Christian.
#* 86% of American adults identified as Christians in 1990 and 76% in 2008.
#* The historic mainline churches and denominations have experienced the steepest declines, while the non-denominational Christian identity has been trending upward, particularly since 2001.
#* The challenge to Christianity in the US does not come from other religions but rather from a rejection of all forms of organized religion.
# 34% of American adults considered themselves "Born Again or Evangelical Christians" in 2008.
# The US population continues to show signs of becoming less religious, with one out of every seven Americans failing to indicate a religious identity in 2008.
#* The "Nones" (no stated religious preference, atheist, or agnostic) continue to grow, though at a much slower pace than in the 1990s, from 8.2% in 1990, to 14.1% in 2001, to 15.0% in 2008.
#* Asian Americans are substantially more likely to indicate no religious identity than other racial or ethnic groups.
# One sign of the lack of attachment of Americans to religion is that 27% do not expect a religious funeral at their death.
# Based on their stated beliefs rather than their religious identification in 2008, 70% of Americans believe in a personal God, roughly 12% of Americans are atheist (no God) or agnostic (unknowable or unsure), and another 12% are deistic (a higher power but no personal God).
# America's religious geography has been transformed since 1990. Religious switching along with Hispanic immigration has significantly changed the religious profile of some states and regions. Between 1990 and 2008, the Catholic population proportion of the New England states fell from 50% to 36% and in New York fell from 44% to 37%, while it rose in California from 29% to 37% and in Texas from 23% to 32%.
# Overall the 1990–2008 ARIS time series shows that changes in religious self-identification in the first decade of the 21st century have been moderate in comparison to the 1990s, which was a period of significant shifts in the religious composition of the United States.

===2024 Barna Group findings===

The media estimates of the number of adult US citizens who consider themselves evangelicals is too high according to 2024 data from the American Worldview Inventory 2024<ref name="crc-acu-2024">{{cite web |title=Cultural Research Center |url=https://www.arizonachristian.edu/culturalresearchcenter/research/ |website=Arizona Christian University |access-date=29 August 2024}}</ref> (AWVI 2024, organized by the Cultural Research Center located at the [[Arizona Christian University]], under the leadership of researcher George Barna). Rather than the conventional estimate of 25% to 40%, only 10% of adult US citizens consider themselves evangelicals, and of that 10% self-identifying as evangelicals, roughly two thirds do not follow major points of Christian Evangelical doctrine.<ref>{{YouTube|hQhK4lXTcK8|Stats Say Evangelical Christianity Losing Ground in America}} CBN News.</ref><ref name="j679">{{cite web | title=Barna, CRC Research finds America's evangelicals are fewer, less biblically grounded and politically disengaged | website=Pathway | date=8 August 2024 | url=https://mbcpathway.com/2024/08/08/barna-crc-research-finds-americas-evangelicals-are-fewer-less-biblically-grounded-and-politically-disengaged/ | access-date=26 August 2024}}</ref><ref>{{cite web|website=Arizona Christian University|url=https://www.arizonachristian.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/CRC-Release-AWVI-4-Aug-6-2024-Fourth-Draft.pdf|title=American Worldview Inventory 2024 (Release #4) New Research Reveals the Limitations of Christian Evangelicalism in American Society|first1=George|last1=Barna|first2=Tracy|last2=Munsil}}</ref>

[[Gallup (company)|Gallup]] survey data found that 73% of Americans were members of a church, synagogue or mosque in 1937, peaking at 76% shortly after [[World War II]], before trending slightly downward to 70% by 2000. The percentage declined steadily during the first two decades of the 21st century, reaching 47% in 2020. Gallup attributed the decline to increasing numbers of Americans expressing no religious preference.<ref>{{cite news |last1=Lardieri |first1=Alexa |title=Church Membership Hits All-Time Low in 2020, Gallup Finds |url=https://www.usnews.com/news/national-news/articles/2021-03-29/church-membership-hits-all-time-low-in-2020-gallup-finds |publisher=US News |date=March 29, 2021 |access-date=March 30, 2021 |archive-date=March 29, 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210329191142/https://www.usnews.com/news/national-news/articles/2021-03-29/church-membership-hits-all-time-low-in-2020-gallup-finds |url-status=live }}</ref><ref>{{cite news |last1=Jones |first1=Jeffrey |title=U.S. Church Membership Falls Below Majority for First Time |url=https://news.gallup.com/poll/341963/church-membership-falls-below-majority-first-time.aspx |publisher=Gallup |date=March 29, 2021 |access-date=March 30, 2021 |archive-date=October 16, 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20211016131919/https://news.gallup.com/poll/341963/church-membership-falls-below-majority-first-time.aspx |url-status=live }}</ref>

A 2013 [[Public Religion Research Institute]] survey reported that 31% of Americans attend [[place of worship|religious services]] at least weekly.<ref>{{cite news| last1=Kaleem| first1=Jaweed| title=Americans Exaggerate How Much They Go To Religious Services, According To Study| url=https://www.huffingtonpost.com/2014/05/17/religious-attendance-exaggeration-survey_n_5344535.html| access-date=May 31, 2014| work=The Huffington Post| date=May 17, 2014| archive-date=May 23, 2014| archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140523105126/http://www.huffingtonpost.com/2014/05/17/religious-attendance-exaggeration-survey_n_5344535.html| url-status=live}} [[margin of error]] of 2.5</ref> According to a 2022 Gallup poll, 75% of Americans report [[Prayer|praying]] often or sometimes and religion plays a very (46%) or fairly (26%) important role in their lives.<ref name="Gallup Poll">{{cite web |date=2022 |title=Religion Historical Trends |url=https://news.gallup.com/poll/1690/religion.aspx |website=Gallup}}</ref>

In a 2009 Gallup survey, 41.6%<ref name=Gallup2009>{{cite web |url=https://www.gallup.com/poll/125999/mississippians-go-church-most-vermonters-least.aspx |title=Mississippians Go to Church the Most; Vermonters, Least |date=February 17, 2010 |publisher=Gallup.com |access-date=March 17, 2012 |archive-date=August 30, 2017 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170830152813/http://www.gallup.com/poll/125999/Mississippians-Go-Church-Most-Vermonters-Least.aspx |url-status=live }}</ref> of American residents stated that they attended a church, synagogue, or mosque once a week or almost every week. This percentage is higher than other surveyed Western countries.<ref name=bbc20070403>{{cite news |title= 'One in 10' attends church weekly |url= https://news.bbc.co.uk/1/hi/uk/6520463.stm |work= BBC News |date= April 3, 2007 |access-date= August 1, 2007 |archive-date= December 22, 2007 |archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20071222011331/http://news.bbc.co.uk/1/hi/uk/6520463.stm |url-status= live }}</ref><ref>[https://www.ncls.org.au/default.aspx?docid=2250&track=82083 NCLS releases latest estimates of church attendance] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20211221055313/http://www.ncls.org.au/default.aspx?docid=2250&track=82083 |date=December 21, 2021 }}, National Church Life Survey, media release, February 28, 2004</ref> [[Church attendance]] varies considerably by state and region. The figures, updated to 2014, ranged from 51% in [[Utah]] to 17% in Vermont.

When it comes to mosque attendance specifically, data collected by a 2017 poll by the Institute for Social Policy and Understanding (ISPU) shows that American Muslim women and men attend the mosque at similar rates (45% for men and 35% for women).<ref name=":2" /> Additionally, when compared to the general public looking at the attendance of religious services, young Muslim Americans attend the mosque at closer rates to older Muslim Americans. Muslim Americans who regularly attend mosques are more likely to work with their neighbors to solve community problems (49 vs. 30 percent), be registered to vote (74 vs. 49 percent), and plan to vote (92 vs. 81 percent). Overall, "there is no correlation between Muslim attitudes toward violence and their frequency of mosque attendance".<ref name=":2" />

==Religion and politics==
{{Main|Religion and politics in the United States}}
{{See also|Religious affiliations of presidents of the United States|Religious affiliation in the United States Senate}}
[[File:Civil marriage is a civil right.JPG|thumb|right|The US guarantees [[freedom of religion]], and some churches in the US take strong stances on political subjects.]]

In August 2010, 67% of Americans said religion was losing influence, compared with 59% who said this in 2006. Majorities of white evangelical Protestants (79%), white mainline Protestants (67%), black Protestants (56%), Catholics (71%), and the religiously unaffiliated (62%) all agreed that religion was losing influence on American life; 53% of the total public said this was a bad thing, while just 10% see it as a good thing.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://pewresearch.org/databank/dailynumber/?NumberID=1079|publisher=Pew Forum on Religion & Public Life|title=Religion Losing Influence in America|access-date=September 16, 2010|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100920003855/https://pewresearch.org/databank/dailynumber/?NumberID=1079|archive-date=September 20, 2010|url-status=dead}}</ref>

Politicians frequently discuss their religion when campaigning, and [[fundamentalist Christianity|fundamentalists]] and black Protestants are highly politically active. However, to keep their status as [[tax-exempt organization]]s they must not officially endorse a candidate. Historically Catholics were heavily [[History of the United States Democratic Party|Democratic]] before the 1970s, while mainline Protestants comprised the core of the [[History of the United States Republican Party|Republican Party]]. Those patterns have faded away—Catholics, for example, now split about 50–50. However, white evangelicals since 1980 have made up a solidly Republican group that favors conservative candidates. Secular voters are increasingly Democratic.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://pewforum.org/docs/?DocID=174 |title=Religion and the 2006 Elections |publisher=Pew Forum |date=December 1, 2006 |access-date=March 17, 2012 |archive-date=April 22, 2010 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100422162848/http://pewforum.org/docs/?DocID=174 |url-status=live }}</ref>

Only four presidential candidates for major parties have been Catholics, all for the Democratic party:
* [[Alfred E. Smith]] in [[1928 United States presidential election|presidential election of 1928]] was subjected to anti-Catholic rhetoric, which seriously hurt him in the Baptist areas of the South and Lutheran areas of the Midwest, but he did well in the Catholic urban strongholds of the Northeast.
* [[John F. Kennedy]] secured the Democratic presidential nomination in 1960. In the [[1960 United States presidential election|1960 election]], Kennedy faced accusations that as a Catholic president he would do as the Pope would tell him to do, a charge that Kennedy refuted in a famous address to Protestant ministers.
* [[John Kerry]], a Catholic, won the Democratic presidential nomination in 2004. In the [[2004 United States presidential election|2004 election]] religion was hardly an issue, and most Catholics voted for his Protestant opponent [[George W. Bush]].<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.nbcnews.com/id/5297138 |title=Exit poll - Decision 2004- NBCNews.com |work=NBC News |access-date=December 29, 2012 |archive-date=September 24, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200924003400/http://www.nbcnews.com/id/5297138 |url-status=dead }}</ref>
* [[Joe Biden]], a Catholic, won the Democratic presidential nomination in 2020, and then won the [[2020 United States presidential election|2020 presidential election]], becoming the second Catholic president, after John F. Kennedy.<ref>{{Cite web|author=NBC News|date=January 19, 2021|title=Biden to become the second Catholic president in U.S. history, after JFK|url=https://www.nbcnews.com/now/video/biden-to-become-the-second-catholic-president-in-u-s-history-after-jfk-99673157918|access-date=January 20, 2021|website=NBC News|language=en|archive-date=January 19, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210119212606/https://www.nbcnews.com/now/video/biden-to-become-the-second-catholic-president-in-u-s-history-after-jfk-99673157918|url-status=live}}</ref> Biden was also the first Catholic vice president.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.npr.org/blogs/politicaljunkie/2009/01/the_first_catholic_vice_presid.html|title=The First Catholic Vice President?|date=January 9, 2009|work=NPR|access-date=March 5, 2015|archive-date=January 14, 2015|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150114120137/http://www.npr.org/blogs/politicaljunkie/2009/01/the_first_catholic_vice_presid.html|url-status=live}}</ref>

[[Joe Lieberman]] was the first major presidential candidate that was Jewish, on the [[Al Gore|Gore]]–Lieberman [[2000 United States presidential election|campaign of 2000]] (although [[John Kerry]] and [[Barry Goldwater]] both had Jewish ancestry, they were practicing Christians). [[Bernie Sanders]] ran against [[Hillary Clinton]] in the Democratic primary of 2016. He was the first major Jewish candidate to compete in the presidential primary process. However, Sanders noted during the campaign that he does not actively practice any religion.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/bernie-sanders-finally-answers-the-god-question/2016/01/26/83429390-bfb0-11e5-bcda-62a36b394160_story.html|title=Why Bernie Sanders doesn't participate in organized religion|date=January 27, 2016|work=www.washingtonpost.com|access-date=December 4, 2016|archive-date=November 10, 2016|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161110184459/https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/bernie-sanders-finally-answers-the-god-question/2016/01/26/83429390-bfb0-11e5-bcda-62a36b394160_story.html|url-status=live}}</ref>

In 2006 [[Keith Ellison]] of [[Minnesota]] became the first Muslim elected to Congress; when [[Quran oath controversy of the 110th United States Congress|re-enacting his swearing-in for photos]], he used the copy of the [[Qur'an]] once owned by Thomas Jefferson.<ref>Michael Isikoff, "I'm a Sunni Muslim", [https://www.msnbc.msn.com/id/16474497/site/newsweek/page/0/ ''Newsweek'' Jan. 4, 2007]{{dead link|date=August 2024|bot=medic}}{{cbignore|bot=medic}}</ref> [[André Carson]] is the second Muslim to serve in Congress.

A Gallup poll released in 2007<ref>{{cite web |url= https://www.gallup.com/poll/26611/Some-Americans-Reluctant-Vote-Mormon-72YearOld-Presidential-Candidates.aspx |title= Some Americans Reluctant to Vote for Mormon, 72-Year-Old Presidential Candidates. But then the number started to drop again and reached record low 43% in 2012 and 40% in 2015.<ref>{{cite web |url= https://www.gallup.com/poll/155285/atheists-muslims-bias-presidential-candidates.aspx |title= Atheists, Muslims See Most Bias as Presidential Candidates |publisher= Gallup News Service |author= Jeffrey M. [[Mitt Romney]], the Republican presidential nominee in 2012, is [[Mormon]] and a member of [[the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints]]. He is the former governor of the state of [[Massachusetts]], and his father [[George W. Romney|George Romney]] was the governor of the state of [[Michigan]].

On January 3, 2013, [[Tulsi Gabbard]] became the first [[Hindu]] member of Congress, using a copy of the [[Bhagavad Gita]] while swearing-in.<ref>{{cite news|last1=Kaleem|first1=Jaweed|title=Tulsi Gabbard, First Hindu In Congress, Uses Bhagavad Gita At Swearing-In|url=https://www.huffingtonpost.com/2013/01/04/tulsi-gabbard-hindu-bhagavad-gita-swearing-in_n_2410078.html|work=HuffPost|access-date=October 17, 2017|date=January 4, 2013|archive-date=January 7, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170107085902/http://www.huffingtonpost.com/2013/01/04/tulsi-gabbard-hindu-bhagavad-gita-swearing-in_n_2410078.html|url-status=live}}</ref>
